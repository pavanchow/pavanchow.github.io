Different types of numbers have different properties. Understanding these helps you avoid bugs and understand AI systems better.

Decimals and Fractions

📝 Same Value, Different Forms

1/2 = 0.5 = 50% 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% 3/4 = 0.75 = 75% 1/3 ≈ 0.333... (repeating) 1/10 = 0.1 = 10%

In AI, we usually work with decimals (floats), but understanding fractions helps with proportions and probabilities.

⚠️ Floating Point Surprise

Computers can't represent all decimals exactly. 0.1 + 0.2 doesn't equal 0.3 in most programming languages—it equals 0.30000000000000004! This is why AI uses tolerance-based comparisons, not exact equality.

🤖 Numbers in AI

Neural networks store weights as floats, often with limited precision (float16, float32). Understanding that small rounding errors accumulate helps you debug numerical instability—a common issue in deep learning.

2 Growth Patterns

How things grow matters enormously in AI. Understanding linear vs. exponential growth helps you predict training times, model sizes, and computational costs.

Linear Growth

Linear growth adds the same amount each step. It's steady and predictable. 

        Linear: Add 10 each time
        
        Step 0:  10     •
        Step 1:  20     • •
        Step 2:  30     • • •
        Step 3:  40     • • • •
        Step 4:  50     • • • • •
        
        Pattern: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50...
        Formula: y = 10x

Exponential Growth

Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each step. It starts slow but explodes. 

        Exponential: Multiply by 2 each time
        
        Step 0:   1     •
        Step 1:   2     • •
        Step 2:   4     • • • •
        Step 3:   8     • • • • • • • •
        Step 4:  16     • • • • • • • • • • • • • • • •
        
        Pattern: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128...
        Formula: y = 2ˣ

💡 The Dramatic Difference

After 10 steps: Linear (adding 10) = 100. Exponential (doubling) = 1,024.
After 20 steps: Linear = 200. Exponential = 1,048,576 (over a million!).
After 30 steps: Linear = 300. Exponential = 1,073,741,824 (over a billion!).

Comparison Chart

📈 Linear Examples

• Pay: $100/hour (more hours = more pay) • Distance: 60 mph (more time = more miles) • Cost: $5/item (more items = higher cost) • Training: loss decreases by fixed amount

📈 Exponential Examples

• Population: doubles every generation • Compound interest: 7% annually • Viral spread: each person infects 2 • Model sizes: 2x each year (GPT growth)

🤖 Growth in AI

Model parameters have grown exponentially: GPT-1 (117M) → GPT-2 (1.5B) → GPT-3 (175B). Training costs grow with model size. Understanding exponential growth helps you grasp why bigger models are so much more expensive—it's not 10x harder, it's 100x or 1000x harder.

3 Orders of Magnitude

AI deals with numbers spanning many orders of magnitude—from tiny probabilities (0.0000001) to billions of parameters. Scientific notation helps us handle these extremes.

Scientific Notation

Scientific notation writes numbers as: a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ a < 10

📝 Large Numbers

1,000 = 10³ = 1e3 (thousand) 1,000,000 = 10⁶ = 1e6 (million) 1,000,000,000 = 10⁹ = 1e9 (billion) 175,000,000,000 = 1.75×10¹¹ = 1.75e11 (GPT-3 parameters)

📝 Small Numbers

0.1 = 10⁻¹ = 1e-1 0.01 = 10⁻² = 1e-2 0.001 = 10⁻³ = 1e-3 (common learning rate) 0.00001 = 10⁻⁵ = 1e-5 (smaller learning rate) 0.0000001 = 10⁻⁷ = 1e-7

Thinking in Orders of Magnitude

💡 Quick Estimation

When numbers differ by an "order of magnitude," one is about 10× the other.

• 100 vs 1,000: 1 order of magnitude difference
• 100 vs 10,000: 2 orders of magnitude
• 1 million vs 1 billion: 3 orders of magnitude

This helps you quickly assess: "Is this change significant?" 

        Scale Comparison (each step = 10×)
        
        │ 10⁰ │ 10¹ │ 10² │ 10³ │ 10⁶ │ 10⁹ │ 10¹² │
        │  1  │ 10  │ 100 │ 1K  │ 1M  │ 1B  │  1T  │
        │     │     │     │     │     │     │      │
        │ one │ ten │     │ kilo│mega │giga │ tera │
        
        Examples:
        • Seconds in a day: ~10⁵ (86,400)
        • Neurons in brain: ~10¹¹ (100 billion)
        • Stars in galaxy: ~10¹¹ (100-400 billion)
        • Atoms in human body: ~10²⁸

AI models are approaching brain-scale parameter counts

🤖 Why This Matters

When someone says a model has "1.5B parameters," you should instantly know that's 1.5 billion = 1.5×10⁹. When a learning rate is "1e-4," that's 0.0001. This fluency lets you read papers, compare models, and tune hyperparameters effectively.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Number types matter: integers for counting, floats for continuous values
  • Linear growth adds constantly; exponential growth multiplies—and explodes
  • Scientific notation (1e6 = 10⁶ = 1 million) handles extreme scales
  • Orders of magnitude let you compare vastly different quantities
  • AI spans from tiny learning rates (10⁻⁵) to trillion-parameter models (10¹²)
